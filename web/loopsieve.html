a sieve of Eratosthenes for 3n+1 loops

Loop Sieve

A second loop, anything other than 1 → 4 → 2 → 1, would be a list of odd numbers that the 3n+1 rule keeps cycling through. This page crosses out numbers that cannot be on such a list, one rule at a time, the way the sieve of Eratosthenes crosses out numbers that cannot be prime. It starts from your rule — if 3n+1 is a power of 2, then n falls straight into the known loop — and follows it as far as it goes. Along the way it measures how much each rule removes, and it shows why only rules that use the size of numbers can cross out more than the multiples of 3.

1 · Your rule: 3n + 1 is a power of 2 pass 1 · proved

Why it works. If n is in a loop, the next odd number S(n) = (3n+1)/2x is in the same loop — a loop is closed under the rule. If 3n + 1 = 2i, the next odd number is 1, whose loop is {1}. So n is in no other loop. Since 2i ≡ 1 (mod 3) only for even i, these are the numbers n = (4k − 1)/3: 5, 21, 85, 341, 1 365, 5 461, 21 845, 87 381, … (OEIS A002450), in binary 101, 10101, 1010101, …. Every third one (21, 1 365, 87 381, …) is a multiple of 3, a dead end that nothing reaches.

They are the doorways into the known loop. On every trajectory that reaches 1, the last odd number before 1 is one of them. Below 220 almost everything enters through 5 (that is the familiar ending … → 5 → 16 → 8 → 4 → 2 → 1):

2 · Pass after pass: cross out whatever lands on a crossed-out number closure · proved

The same argument repeats. If the next odd number of n is already crossed out, then n is crossed out too: a loop through n would pass through its next odd number. Pass 1 is your rule. Pass 2 crosses out the numbers that land on a pass-1 number, such as 3 → 5, 13 → 5, 113 → 85, 227 → 341. In closed form they are (2x(4k − 1) − 3)/9, with x even when k ≡ 1 and odd when k ≡ 2 (mod 3). Pass d crosses out exactly the numbers that reach 1 in d odd steps. The multiples of 3 are crossed out before anything else (section 3), and 1 itself is the known loop.

A fixed number of passes removes almost nothing

Proved: a polylogarithmic sliver. Along a path of d odd steps from n to 1 with X halvings, 2X = 3d n ∏(1 + 1/(3nᵢ)) ≤ 4d n. So a number below N crossed out at pass d has X < log₂N + 2d, and there are at most C(⌊log₂N⌋ + 2d, d) such halving patterns. Any fixed number of passes crosses out a share that shrinks to zero as N grows. To clear everything below N takes as many passes as the slowest number: 65 below 210, 195 below 220.

This is the backward tree of 1, drawn on the main page with the closed form of every path back from 1. Its verdict stands here too. A second loop would sit in its own part of the graph, which no path back from 1 ever reaches. So running the passes to the end is the same as checking that every number reaches 1, which is the conjecture itself.

3 · The dead ends: multiples of 3 T0 · proved

3m + 1 always leaves remainder 1 when divided by 3. So no odd number ever maps to a multiple of 3, and a multiple of 3 has no predecessor in any loop. This is the only rule on the page that removes a fixed share, a third of all odd numbers, at every size and without following a single orbit. The multiples of 3 also reach 1 like everything else, so the passes would cross them out anyway at their own pass. In the grid they are hatched from the start.

4 · Where the passes stop: 271, and the shadow above it cited · computed

By computer, every odd number below 271 ≈ 2.36 × 1021 reaches 1 (Barina 2025). So every pass of this sieve has already been run on every number below that size, and every member of a second loop would have to be larger. Above 271 a number is crossed out as soon as its orbit dips below 271. Your rule relaxed is the first step of this: 3n + 1 = 2i · s with s < 271. The chart below shows, for numbers h bits above 271, the share whose orbit has dipped within k odd steps. It is computed exactly from halving patterns. Each pattern of total X halvings is one residue class, holding a share 2−X of the odd numbers, and it dips at step j when Xj > h + j·log₂3.

The stragglers never all go. For every height the share still standing shrinks to zero, because an odd step loses 2 − log₂3 ≈ 0.415 bits on average. It shrinks like 0.9465k, where 0.9465 = λ/3 and λ = αα/(α−1)α−1 with α = log₂3. That is the same constant as the project's tail-rate bound, because both count the same halving patterns. But the stragglers are whole residue classes: n ≡ −1 (mod 2j+1) rises j times in a row before anything can dip. So the shadow is a statement about almost all numbers, and a loop is a finite set, which "almost all" never excludes.

5 · Why remainders alone cannot cross out anything else proved · computed

Rules like "multiples of 3 are out" look only at remainders. It is natural to hunt for more of them: maybe some class mod 16, or mod 36, never holds a loop member. None exists that can be proved from remainders alone. The reason is that 3n+1 has loops among fractions with odd denominators, one for every halving pattern: −1 → −1, −5 → −7 → −5, 1/5 → 1/5, −19/11 → −23/11 → −29/11 → −19/11, … Remainders make sense for these fractions, and they pass through every class except the multiples of 3. An argument that used only remainders would apply to them word for word and cross them out, but they exist. So every further rule has to use how big the numbers are, as the passes and the shadow do.

Proof. Mod 2a: a class fixes the first few halvings of an odd number, say x₁, …, xj, and then only says the next halving count is at least t. The loop with halving pattern (x₁, …, xj, t) repeated starts in that class. Mod 3b: after b odd steps the remainder mod 3b depends only on the last b halving counts. Every class prime to 3 arises this way, because every such number has chains of predecessors of any length. Putting the 2-adic pattern first and the 3-adic one last gives a single loop that lands in both classes. ∎

6 · Largest and smallest: roles, not membership pointers

The strongest filters in this project do not cross numbers out of loops at all. They restrict which member can be the largest or the smallest, and they use size through the order: the largest member falls on its next step, the smallest rises. At depth 1, 2, 4 and 8 the largest-member gate passes 11.1 %, 5.6 %, 1.8 % and 0.4 % of odd numbers, and the smallest-member gate 33 %, 25 %, 13.5 % and 6.0 % (the main page and the drop function). Every loop needs one number of each kind, but a number that fails both can still sit in the middle of a loop.

What can finish has to use size together with the arithmetic of 2 and 3. The results that do are about the length of a loop, not its members. A second loop needs at least 1.375 × 1011 odd members (Hercher 2023, the multiplicative ledger), and it cannot be made of 91 or fewer runs of rises.
