3n+1 loops whose halving pattern hugs the straight line

Near-Balanced Loops

A loop of 3n+1 with L odd members and B halvings has a halving pattern: how many times each member is halved. Drawn as a staircase, the pattern climbs from (0, 0) to (L, B). The most balanced pattern, the Christoffel word, stays within one step of the straight line between the endpoints. Kevin Knight proved that this loop never consists of whole numbers. This page shows that the same is true one and two swaps away from balance, and for a whole run of corners moved together, by moving the loop equation into the number field built from the L-th root of 2. A second, simpler argument about repeated stretches of the pattern then reaches much further for long loops.

The idea in one line proved

The loop's first member is c/d with d = 2B − 3L (Böhm and Sontacchi). Let θ = 21/L. When L and B share no factor, dividing by d is the same as dividing by θB − 3 in the ring Z[θ], because 2B − 3L is exactly the norm of θB − 3. In that ring, c becomes a sum of powers of 2 read off how far the staircase strays from the straight line. A loop exists only if 2B − 3L divides the norm of that sum. When the norm is smaller than 2B − 3L, the loop cannot exist.

  • Perfect balance. The sum is a geometric series whose value is a unit, because 2 − 1 = 1. Its norm is a power of 2, so no balanced loop exists. This is Knight's theorem, by a second route.
  • One swap away. Swapping two neighbouring letters of the balanced pattern leaves a three-term expression, θe+1 − θe + 1 or θk − θ + 1. Parseval's identity bounds its norm by (41/9)L/2 ≈ 2.13L, while 2B − 3L > 2.56L once L ≥ 18.
  • Two swaps away. Each swap moves one corner of the staircase by one step, so two swaps leave a five-term expression. At the smallest B, Parseval bounds its norm by about 8.46L/2 ≈ 2.91L, once the mixed case, one corner up and one down, is multiplied by 1 + θ/2, whose norm is almost exactly 1. That is below 2B − 3L ≈ 3L. The gap is small, so it takes Rhin's lower bound for 2B − 3L beyond L = 4000 and an exact check below it.
  • A run of levels. Swaps are not the right unit. Give each corner a level, its distance below the straight line. The sum changes only where two corners that are neighbours in level are moved differently. So raising a whole run of consecutive levels leaves three terms, 1 − θg + θf, and the one-swap proof applies. This rules out some patterns far from balance: sets of swaps as far as about 0.2·L swaps away, at the smallest B, and patterns containing a 3, which no swaps reach.
  • Repeats. A second argument needs no number field. If the same stretch of halving counts appears twice in a loop, the two members where it starts differ by a multiple of 2X, where X is the number of halvings in the stretch. But the members of a loop whose staircase stays near the line are small. Patterns near balance are full of long repeats, so none of them is a loop once L is large compared with the number of moved corners. At the shortest length a loop can have, this excludes about 400 million moved corners.

Raise a run of levels exact, in your browser

The number at each corner is its level: how far the corner sits below the straight line, in steps of 1/L. Pick a run of consecutive levels and every corner in it moves up one step. The test number then has three terms however long the run is. Corners with the highest levels sit inside a 1, 2 pair, and raising one is the swap to 2, 1. Corners with lower levels sit inside 2, 2, and raising one makes 3, 1.

Repeats: the same stretch twice exact, in your browser

Suppose the same stretch of halving counts appears at two places in a loop, with X halvings in all. Then the two members where the stretches start differ by a multiple of 2X. In the real loop 319 → 485 → 367 → 557 → 421 of 3n+13 the pattern is 1, 2, 1, 2, 2. The stretch 1, 2 starts at 319 and again at 367, and 319 − 367 = −48 is a multiple of 23. On the other hand every member of a loop of 3n+1 is at most 2σ+τ. Here σ is the height spread, which measures how far the staircase strays from the line, and τ = −log₂(2B/L − 3) depends only on L and B. So a loop has no repeated stretch with X ≥ σ + τ. The theorems proved · cited · computed

Theorem 1. Let gcd(L, B) = 1 and log₂3 < B/L < 2. No pattern obtained from the Christoffel word of slope B/L by exchanging two neighbouring, different letters is the halving pattern of a loop of positive whole numbers.
Proof sketch. 1. Z[θ]/(θB − 3) ≅ Z/d, with θ going to 3u2−v where uB − vL = 1. 2. Modulo θB − 3, c ≡ θB(L−1)·Σ θ−Di, where Di/L is the height of the line above the staircase at step i. 3. For the balanced word that sum is θ1−L/(θ − 1), a unit. A swap changes a single Di by ±L, which leaves θe+1 − θe + 1 for a 12 → 21 swap and θk − θ + 1 for a 21 → 12 swap, up to units. 4. A loop would make d divide the norm of that element. All its exponents are at most L − b, so Parseval and AM–GM give a norm below (41/9)L/2. 5. d > 2.56L for L ≥ 18 (Knight's Lemma 3.3, from Ellison 1971), so d cannot divide it. Lengths up to 17 are checked directly: all 159 internal swaps, none integral. ∎
Theorem 2. Under the same conditions, no pattern two swaps from a balanced pattern is the halving pattern of a loop of positive whole numbers. Together with Theorem 1, no loop staircase misses the balanced corners in only one or two places by one step each.
Proof sketch. 1. A swap of two neighbouring letters moves exactly one corner of the staircase up or down by one step, so two swaps move two different corners. A raised corner must be a 12 corner and a lowered one a 21 corner, except when the two corners are neighbours. A raise next to a lowering would need a letter 0 or 3. 2. Moving a corner at level D adds θ−D to the sum when it goes up and subtracts θ−D/2 when it goes down. Up to units this leaves five terms: 1 − θe₁ + θe₁+1 − θe₂ + θe₂+1 for two raises, θk₁ − θ + 1 − θg+1 + θg for two lowerings, and 1 − θ + θk − θt + θt+1 for one of each. 3. Parseval bounds each norm by FL/2, after the mixed family is multiplied by 1 + θ/2. Up to a factor that tends to 1, F is at most 0.9404 · 4B/L, and at most 8.46 at the smallest B. The norm of 1 + θ/2 is 1 − (−1)L21−L, so the factor costs almost nothing. 4. For B ≥ ⌊L log₂3⌋ + 2, d > 2B−1 beats the bound once L ≥ 100. For B = ⌊L log₂3⌋ + 1, Rhin's 1987 bound B log 2 − L log 3 ≥ B−13.3, which gives 2B − 3L > 3LB−13.3, beats it once L ≥ 4000. 5. The 3 622 remaining slopes are checked exactly: 3 602 by comparing d with the bound, and 20, all with L ≤ 147, by computing the sum modulo d for every pair of moved corners. ∎
Theorem 3. Under the same conditions, give each corner of the balanced staircase its level, the distance below the straight line in steps of 1/L. Raise by one step every corner whose level lies in a run of consecutive levels. If the result is a pattern, it is not the halving pattern of a loop of positive whole numbers. Runs made of 12 corners are sets of swaps, exactly min(g, f − g) swaps from balance, which reaches about 0.2·L at the smallest B. The other runs give patterns that contain a 3. The condition that the result be a pattern matters: for L = 5, B = 8 the run of levels 1 and 2 gives 1, 3, 0, 3, 1, which is not a pattern, and it does satisfy the loop equation.
Proof sketch. 1. For any pattern, let m count how many steps each corner sits above its balanced position, and list the corners by level. Then (θ − 1)θL−1 times the sum has, as the coefficient of θj, the difference of 2m between two neighbouring levels. 2. So a term appears only where neighbouring levels are moved differently. A run raised by one step has two such places, and the test number is 1 − θg + θf, where g is L − 1 minus the top level and f is L minus the bottom level. 3. The result is a pattern exactly when every corner of the run is followed by a 2, which means its level is at least 2L − B, or when the run reaches the top level, which gives a rotation of the balanced pattern. In the first case f ≤ B − L. 4. Parseval bounds the norm by (1 + 4g/L + 4f/L)L/2. At the smallest B this is below 5.69L/2 < 2.56L for L ≥ 18, and Ellison's bound finishes. For larger B it is below 2B−1. Lengths up to 17 are checked directly: 2 218 patterns, none a loop. ∎
Theorem 4. Take a loop of 3n+1 with L odd members and B halvings, with or without a common factor. A stretch of halving counts that occurs at two different places has fewer than σ + τ halvings, where σ is the height spread of the staircase and τ = −log₂(2B/L − 3). Two consequences follow. A pattern that differs from a balanced one in k corners, each by one step, is not a loop once k + 1 < L/Ψ, with Ψ = 2 + (4 + log₂(L/3) + 13.3 log₂(L log₂3 + 1))/log₂3. And a pattern within three swaps of balance is never a loop, for every L.
Proof sketch. 1. If the halving counts from two members n and n′ agree for j steps with X halvings, subtracting the two chains of equations gives 3j(n − n′) = 2X(nj − n′j). So 2X divides n − n′, and the largest member exceeds 2X. This is the lemma of Terras and Everett. 2. The loop equation gives n ≤ 2σ/(2B/L − 3) for every member. For the smallest member this is Belaga's bound. 3. So X < σ + τ. Rhin's bound gives τ < log₂(L/3) + 13.3 log₂ B at the smallest B. For larger B, τ < log₂ L − 1 with no deep input. 4. A balanced pattern has at most j + 1 different stretches of length j. Each moved corner spoils at most j + 1 starting places. If more than j + 1 places are left, two of them carry the same stretch, and step 3 is violated once the stretch is long enough. 5. For three corners, Ellison's bound settles every L ≥ 77. An exact check settles the pairs with 30 ≤ L ≤ 76, and the 1 087 329 cases with L ≤ 29 are checked directly. So this case does not need Rhin's bound. ∎
Checked by machine. Steps 1 to 3 are proved in Lean 4, in lean/Collatz/Repeat.lean, with no sorry and no Mathlib. Lean's core has no real numbers, so τ and σ enter as whole-number tests. One is a whole number N with (3·2N + 1)L ≤ 2NL + B, which says N ≥ τ. The other is a whole number D at least as large as the height of the largest member above the lowest level of the staircase. The value D = σ·L always qualifies. The checked statement is (X + 1)·L < N·L + D, which is X + 1 < N + D/L. Because N is whole, this can be one halving weaker than the theorem as stated on paper. An exact form, with a fraction in place of 2N, is checked as well. Steps 4 and 5 are not in Lean and are checked by the Python tests. Theorems 1 to 3 need a number field and are not in Lean either.

A corollary. A loop whose largest member is less than 1.86 times its smallest would have to be balanced, so by Knight's theorem every loop other than 1 → 4 → 2 → 1 spreads by at least that factor.

It really is about 3n+1. For 3n+13, where 28 − 35 = 13, both kinds of loop exist: the balanced 319 → 485 → 367 → 557 → 421 and the one-swap 283 → 431 → 653 → 493 → 373. That is Knight's Figure 1, multiplied by 13.

Every two-swap pattern is ruled out in all four cases, as Theorem 2 now proves for every length. Patterns far from balance have large norms, as random patterns do. So the method rules out patterns close to balance, proved at one and two swaps, rather than every loop. At three swaps the method stops. The Parseval masses reach 15.9, or 12.8 after the same flattening, above the 9 it needs. A sharper estimate cannot settle every three-swap pattern either: at L = 233 one of them has an odd norm 11 times 2B − 3L. It is not a loop, but its size cannot show that. The repeat argument takes over there. It rules out every pattern within three swaps of balance, for every length and without the coprime condition, and many more swaps for long loops. What neither method reaches is a pattern with no long repeats, which is what a random pattern looks like.

Where this sits literature

  • Balanced loops are Knight's. K. Knight, Collatz high cycles do not exist, Discrete Mathematics 349 (2026) 114812. His proof uses the reversal symmetry of Christoffel words. Halbeisen and Hungerbühler (1997) had shown this loop has the largest smallest member for its counts.
  • Mirror image of the circuit results. Steiner (1977) and Simons–de Weger (2005) rule out loops made of a few long runs, the least balanced kind, using Baker's theory. This result rules out loops next to perfect balance.
  • The repeat argument is built from classical parts. Terras (1976) and Everett (1977) showed that whole numbers with the same first k parities agree modulo 2k. Belaga (2003) bounded the smallest member of a loop by 1/(2B/L − 3). Rhin (1987) gave the lower bound for 2B − 3L.
  • Novelty. The number-field form, the one- and two-swap theorems, the run theorem, the repeat theorem with its corollaries and the spread corollary are not in Knight's paper or the related work found so far. The repeat argument is short enough to be folklore. A wider literature search is still needed before calling any of this new mathematics.
