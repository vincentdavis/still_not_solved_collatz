3n+1 loops whose halving pattern hugs the straight line

Near-Balanced Loops

A loop of 3n+1 with L odd members and B halvings has a halving pattern: how many times each member is halved. Drawn as a staircase, the pattern climbs from (0, 0) to (L, B). The most balanced pattern, the Christoffel word, stays within one step of the straight line between the endpoints. Kevin Knight proved that this loop never consists of whole numbers. This page shows that the same is true one and two swaps away from balance, by moving the loop equation into the number field built from the L-th root of 2.

The idea in one line proved

The loop's first member is c/d with d = 2B − 3L (Böhm and Sontacchi). Let θ = 21/L. When L and B share no factor, dividing by d is the same as dividing by θB − 3 in the ring Z[θ], because 2B − 3L is exactly the norm of θB − 3. In that ring, c becomes a sum of powers of 2 read off how far the staircase strays from the straight line. A loop exists only if 2B − 3L divides the norm of that sum. When the norm is smaller than 2B − 3L, the loop cannot exist.

  • Perfect balance. The sum is a geometric series whose value is a unit, because 2 − 1 = 1. Its norm is a power of 2, so no balanced loop exists. This is Knight's theorem, by a second route.
  • One swap away. Swapping two neighbouring letters of the balanced pattern leaves a three-term expression, θe+1 − θe + 1 or θk − θ + 1. Parseval's identity bounds its norm by (41/9)L/2 ≈ 2.13L, while 2B − 3L > 2.56L once L ≥ 18.
  • Two swaps away. Each swap moves one corner of the staircase by one step, so two swaps leave a five-term expression. At the smallest B, Parseval bounds its norm by about 8.46L/2 ≈ 2.91L, once the mixed case, one corner up and one down, is multiplied by 1 + θ/2, whose norm is almost exactly 1. That is below 2B − 3L ≈ 3L. The gap is small, so it takes Rhin's lower bound for 2B − 3L beyond L = 4000 and an exact check below it.

The theorems proved · cited · computed

Theorem 1. Let gcd(L, B) = 1 and log₂3 < B/L < 2. No pattern obtained from the Christoffel word of slope B/L by exchanging two neighbouring, different letters is the halving pattern of a loop of positive whole numbers.
Proof sketch. 1. Z[θ]/(θB − 3) ≅ Z/d, with θ going to 3u2−v where uB − vL = 1. 2. Modulo θB − 3, c ≡ θB(L−1)·Σ θ−Di, where Di/L is the height of the line above the staircase at step i. 3. For the balanced word that sum is θ1−L/(θ − 1), a unit. A swap changes a single Di by ±L, which leaves θe+1 − θe + 1 for a 12 → 21 swap and θk − θ + 1 for a 21 → 12 swap, up to units. 4. A loop would make d divide the norm of that element. All its exponents are at most L − b, so Parseval and AM–GM give a norm below (41/9)L/2. 5. d > 2.56L for L ≥ 18 (Knight's Lemma 3.3, from Ellison 1971), so d cannot divide it. Lengths up to 17 are checked directly: all 159 internal swaps, none integral. ∎
Theorem 2. Under the same conditions, no pattern two swaps from a balanced pattern is the halving pattern of a loop of positive whole numbers. Together with Theorem 1, no loop staircase misses the balanced corners in only one or two places by one step each.
Proof sketch. 1. A swap of two neighbouring letters moves exactly one corner of the staircase up or down by one step, so two swaps move two different corners. A raised corner must be a 12 corner and a lowered one a 21 corner, except when the two corners are neighbours. A raise next to a lowering would need a letter 0 or 3. 2. Moving a corner at level D adds θ−D to the sum when it goes up and subtracts θ−D/2 when it goes down. Up to units this leaves five terms: 1 − θe₁ + θe₁+1 − θe₂ + θe₂+1 for two raises, θk₁ − θ + 1 − θg+1 + θg for two lowerings, and 1 − θ + θk − θt + θt+1 for one of each. 3. Parseval bounds each norm by FL/2, after the mixed family is multiplied by 1 + θ/2. Up to a factor that tends to 1, F is at most 0.9404 · 4B/L, and at most 8.46 at the smallest B. The norm of 1 + θ/2 is 1 − (−1)L21−L, so the factor costs almost nothing. 4. For B ≥ ⌊L log₂3⌋ + 2, d > 2B−1 beats the bound once L ≥ 100. For B = ⌊L log₂3⌋ + 1, Rhin's 1987 bound B log 2 − L log 3 ≥ B−13.3, which gives 2B − 3L > 3LB−13.3, beats it once L ≥ 4000. 5. The 3 622 remaining slopes are checked exactly: 3 602 by comparing d with the bound, and 20, all with L ≤ 147, by computing the sum modulo d for every pair of moved corners. ∎

A corollary. A loop whose largest member is less than 1.86 times its smallest would have to be balanced, so by Knight's theorem every loop other than 1 → 4 → 2 → 1 spreads by at least that factor.

It really is about 3n+1. For 3n+13, where 28 − 35 = 13, both kinds of loop exist: the balanced 319 → 485 → 367 → 557 → 421 and the one-swap 283 → 431 → 653 → 493 → 373. That is Knight's Figure 1, multiplied by 13.

Every two-swap pattern is ruled out in all four cases, as Theorem 2 now proves for every length. Patterns far from balance have large norms, as random patterns do. So the method rules out patterns close to balance, proved at one and two swaps, rather than every loop. At three swaps the method stops. The Parseval masses reach 15.9, or 12.8 after the same flattening, above the 9 it needs. A sharper estimate cannot settle every three-swap pattern either: at L = 233 one of them has an odd norm 11 times 2B − 3L. It is not a loop, but its size cannot show that.

Where this sits literature

  • Balanced loops are Knight's. K. Knight, Collatz high cycles do not exist, Discrete Mathematics 349 (2026) 114812. His proof uses the reversal symmetry of Christoffel words. Halbeisen and Hungerbühler (1997) had shown this loop has the largest smallest member for its counts.
  • Mirror image of the circuit results. Steiner (1977) and Simons–de Weger (2005) rule out loops made of a few long runs, the least balanced kind, using Baker's theory. This result rules out loops next to perfect balance.
  • Novelty. The number-field form, the one- and two-swap theorems and the spread corollary are not in Knight's paper or the related work found so far. A wider literature search is still needed before calling them new mathematics.
